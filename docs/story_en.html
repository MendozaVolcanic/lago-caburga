The lake, the data and the conflict

What the evidence actually says about the decline of Lake Caburga — and how other places resolved the very same dispute.

Start ↓
01 · The physics

A lake that drains into the earth

Lake Caburga (Pucón, Araucanía, Chile) is unique in the country: it has no surface outlet river. Its water seeps underground through the "Ojos del Caburga". That makes it exceptionally sensitive to any change in its inflow.

Think of a bathtub with the plug at the bottom, not the rim. A normal lake stops dropping at its outlet river's level. Caburga can keep emptying far below that, because its drain is underneath. A deficit that would be mild elsewhere is amplified here.

02 · The conflict

Two narratives, 18 years apart

The community blames a dike that in 2007 cut off a branch of the Trafampulli river. The authorities blame the megadrought. The dispute reached the Supreme Court — but no ruling has resolved it.

Aerial photos from 1979, 1994, 1998 and 2007 show the branch active. In 2005, residents of neighboring Lake Colico claimed a private owner had diverted it. The water authority (DGA) ordered a dike built to "restore the natural course" toward Colico. The branch to Caburga dried up.

In May 2022, after 16 years of unanswered petitions, a local Mapuche community tore down the dike by hand in four days. The water returned. In August 2025 the Supreme Court authorized rebuilding it; by December 2025 the DGA announced the closure again, and communities are opposing it.

03 · Method & data

How we investigated this — with open data

This study doesn't start from a position. It starts from public data and the two university reports. The whole chain is reproducible: code and data are open.

We used three independent precipitation sources — CR2 weather stations, ERA5 satellite reanalysis via Open-Meteo (1950-2025), and the gridded CR2MET product — plus neighboring river flows, satellite snow cover, and Sentinel-2 imagery.

Methodological honesty

Different data products give different magnitudes. The CR2 station (direct measurement, at altitude) detects a −34% drop; the coarser ERA5 reanalysis smooths it to −7%. For mountain terrain, direct measurement is more reliable — but we show both. A Mann-Kendall test confirms the CR2 decline is statistically significant (p=0.0005).

Our position

The first-order cause is the megadrought. The dike is a real but secondary aggravating factor — on the order of 20%.

The clearest proof: when El Niño arrived in 2024, the lake recovered +350 m of shoreline without removing the dike. If the dike were the main cause, that couldn't have happened. But "secondary" is not "irrelevant": in a drying climate, closing off one of the few inflows the lake still receives is a decision the evidence does not support as necessary.

05 · The way out

How others unlocked the same conflict

Caburga is not the world's first lake caught between drought and human intervention. The cases that were resolved share a pattern — and so do the ones that stalled. The good news: there is a proven path.

The model that worked is Mono Lake (California): identical story (a city diverted the inflows, the lake dropped 14 m, the ecosystem collapsed), resolved not by winning a lawsuit but with three things — an agreed target level, independent science with permanent monitoring, and regular meetings of all parties. The aqueduct still runs, but regulated to reach that level.

Six steps for Caburga: (1) shared transparent data; (2) a measurable target level instead of blame; (3) real flow gauges on the Río Blanco, Trafampulli and the Ojos; (4) legal protection of the lake (as Aculeo, declared an Urban Wetland in 2025); (5) whole-basin management; (6) using the El Niño window to reach agreement calmly. See the full proposal.

This study takes a position because the evidence allows it. But its goal is not to win the argument — it is to give the community, the authorities and scientists a common ground of data, so the decision can be made calmly while there is still water to discuss it.
